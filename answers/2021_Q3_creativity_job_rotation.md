## Question

Job rotation refers to "the periodic shifting of an employee from one task to another" (Robbins & Judge, 2019, p. 289). Managers tend to believe that job rotation is beneficial to employee creativity. Drawing on one theory covered in the Creativity, Innovation, and Innovation Diffusion class, explain the pros and cons of job rotation for employee creativity.

## Full Essay Answer

Job rotation — the periodic shifting of an employee from one task to another, sometimes also called cross-training — is widely practised in organisations as a means to reduce boredom, develop employee capabilities, and broaden organisational knowledge. Managers commonly assume that this breadth of experience will naturally enhance employee creativity. However, the relationship between job rotation and creativity is more nuanced than this assumption suggests. The **Dual-Pathway to Creativity Model** (Nijstad, De Dreu, Rietzschel, & Baas, 2010) provides a sophisticated theoretical framework for evaluating both the benefits and the costs of job rotation for creative output.

### What Is Creativity?

Creativity refers to the ability to produce ideas, solutions, or products that are both **novel** (original, unexpected) and **useful** (of value to the organisation or its stakeholders). It is a critical individual-level outcome in modern organisations, as it underpins innovation, problem solving, and adaptability to change.

### The Dual-Pathway to Creativity Model: Core Tenets

Nijstad et al. (2010) propose that creativity can be reached via two distinct cognitive pathways, and that individuals need both to reach peak creative performance:

1. **Cognitive Flexibility** — the use of broad and inclusive cognitive categories in idea generation, through flexible switching among these categories and through the use of **remote associations** between them. A cognitively flexible thinker draws on diverse domains of knowledge, makes unexpected connections between distant ideas, and can rapidly shift perspective. Cognitive flexibility is associated with breadth of knowledge and the capacity to think "outside the box." It tends to produce a larger quantity of diverse ideas.

2. **Cognitive Persistence** — the systematic, effortful, and in-depth exploration of a few cognitive categories in idea generation. A cognitively persistent thinker digs deeply into a narrow domain, carefully developing and refining ideas, and sustaining focused attention over time. Cognitive persistence is associated with depth of knowledge and expertise, and tends to produce more thoroughly developed, refined ideas.

Crucially, the model argues that creativity ideally requires **both pathways simultaneously** — cognitive flexibility to generate novel combinations of knowledge from diverse domains, and cognitive persistence to develop and refine those ideas into something practically useful. An individual with flexibility but no persistence may generate many superficial ideas; an individual with persistence but no flexibility may produce refined but conventional solutions.

### Application to Job Rotation

**The Benefit: Job Rotation Increases Cognitive Flexibility**

Job rotation exposes employees to different tasks, functions, roles, and areas of the organisation. As employees accumulate knowledge and experience across diverse work domains, they build a broader and more varied **knowledge base**. This breadth of knowledge is the foundation of cognitive flexibility: having encountered different problems, processes, and perspectives, the rotated employee is better positioned to draw **remote associations** — unexpected connections between knowledge drawn from different fields or functions.

For example, an engineer who rotates through product design, manufacturing, and customer service roles may draw on insights from all three when tasked with a creative problem-solving challenge — combining understanding of user needs, production constraints, and design possibilities in ways that a non-rotated specialist might not. This interdisciplinarity is the mechanism by which job rotation can enhance creativity via the cognitive flexibility pathway.

**The Cost: Job Rotation Decreases Cognitive Persistence**

The same exposure that broadens an employee's knowledge base also comes at a cost: **depth of knowledge** in any one domain is reduced. Employees have finite time and cognitive resources. When that time is distributed across multiple roles through job rotation, they inevitably develop shallower expertise in each individual area. Depth of expertise is precisely what underpins cognitive persistence — the capacity for the systematic, in-depth exploration of a problem domain required to develop creative ideas to a high level of quality.

A rotated employee may therefore be capable of generating a wide range of novel, cross-domain ideas, but lack the depth of understanding in any one field to develop those ideas rigorously or to recognise which are feasible. This constitutes a real cost to the **cognitive persistence** pathway of creativity.

In short: job rotation expands the breadth of an employee's knowledge (boosting cognitive flexibility) but necessarily reduces the depth of their knowledge in any given domain (reducing cognitive persistence). The net effect on creativity will therefore depend on the relative demands of the creative task. Tasks requiring novel, divergent ideation across domains may benefit from job rotation; tasks requiring deep, sustained creative problem-solving within a specialist domain may suffer from it.

### Supporting Framework: Bounded Rationality

The trade-off identified above is consistent with **bounded rationality** theory (Simon, 1955), which holds that human cognitive capacity is limited. Individuals cannot optimise in all dimensions simultaneously — they must satisfice, making reasonable trade-offs within their cognitive constraints. Job rotation forces employees to allocate limited cognitive resources across multiple domains, inevitably incurring opportunity costs. Gaining breadth comes at the expense of depth; this cognitive constraint underlies the tension between cognitive flexibility and cognitive persistence identified by the dual-pathway model.

### Conclusion

The Dual-Pathway to Creativity Model reveals that managers' optimistic belief in job rotation as uniformly beneficial for creativity is overstated. Job rotation enhances **cognitive flexibility** by broadening the employee's knowledge base and enabling remote associations across domains — a genuine benefit for creative ideation. However, it simultaneously reduces **cognitive persistence** by sacrificing depth of expertise in any one domain — a genuine cost for the development and refinement of creative ideas. The optimal approach may depend on the type of creativity required: for roles demanding innovative combination of ideas across disciplines, job rotation is beneficial; for roles requiring deep creative mastery of a single domain, it may be counterproductive. Managers would be well-advised to match job rotation policies to the specific nature of the creative demands they wish to foster.
