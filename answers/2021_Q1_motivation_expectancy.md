# 2021 Q1: Motivation — Expectancy Theory

## Question

A company, "Octopus", offers significantly higher salaries to its employees than other companies in the same industry with a belief that high salaries motivate employees. Explain Expectancy Theory in order to discuss why high salaries alone may not be sufficient to increase motivation. In doing so, discuss additional organisational practices that can further increase employee motivation.

*Note that whenever you draw on a theory, you should explain the basic tenets of the theory.* [100%]

---

## Full Essay Answer

Octopus's belief that high salaries alone motivate employees reflects a common but incomplete understanding of motivation. While salary is a relevant factor, motivation is a far more complex process that cannot be reduced to a single variable. **Expectancy Theory**, first proposed by Victor Vroom, provides the most useful framework for understanding why high salaries alone are insufficient, and for identifying additional practices that can increase motivation.

### Expectancy Theory: Core Tenets

Expectancy Theory proposes that motivation is determined by three distinct perceptual factors, and that all three must be strong simultaneously for an employee to be motivated (Robbins & Judge, 2024):

1. **Expectancy** — the belief that exerting effort will lead to effective performance. The key question an employee asks is: "If I try hard, will I actually perform well?" This perception depends on the employee's confidence in their abilities, the quality of training they have received, the clarity of performance standards, and access to adequate resources and support.

2. **Instrumentality** — the belief that achieving good performance will lead to a desired reward or outcome. The key question is: "If I perform well, will the organisation actually reward me?" This depends on the perceived clarity and reliability of the link between performance levels and the rewards distributed by the organisation. If employees do not believe that high performance will be recognised or rewarded differentially, instrumentality is low.

3. **Valence** — the degree to which the available rewards satisfy the employee's personal needs, goals, and values. The key question is: "Do I actually care about this reward?" High valence exists when the reward is aligned with what the employee values most.

Crucially, Expectancy Theory treats these three factors as **multiplicative** rather than additive — motivation is a function of Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence. This means that if **any one** of the three factors is zero (or near zero), overall motivation collapses to zero, regardless of how high the other two factors are. A high salary cannot compensate for weak expectancy or instrumentality.

### Why High Salaries Alone Are Insufficient

Octopus's high salaries may improve **Valence** — employees who value monetary rewards will find a high salary highly desirable. However, this improvement in valence alone does not guarantee motivation.

First, consider **Expectancy**: an employee who doubts their ability to perform well — perhaps because they lack the skills, training, or resources needed for the job — will not exert high effort even if they believe that high performance would eventually be rewarded. The effort–performance link is broken. A high salary does nothing to repair this.

Second, consider **Instrumentality**: if employees do not perceive a clear connection between their performance levels and the salary they receive — for instance, if pay raises are given on the basis of seniority, favouritism, or tenure rather than performance — then even a high base salary does not create a credible performance–reward link. Employees will see no point in going above and beyond if they cannot see how their performance differentially affects their rewards.

In this way, Octopus's policy of offering high salaries addresses only one dimension of the motivation equation (valence), while leaving expectancy and instrumentality potentially weak. The result is that employees may feel well-compensated but still lack the drive to exert effort and perform at their best.

### Additional Organisational Practices

To address all three components of Expectancy Theory, Octopus should consider the following additional practices:

**Improving Expectancy:**

- **Training and development:** Investing in formal training sessions, online learning programmes, and coaching helps employees build the skills and competencies they need to perform effectively. This directly strengthens the effort–performance link by increasing employees' confidence in their own abilities. Self-efficacy theory (Robbins & Judge, 2024) supports this: as employees experience enactive mastery (successfully completing tasks) through training, their self-efficacy and expectancy both increase, creating a virtuous cycle.
- **Clear performance standards:** Employees should receive clear information about what performance levels are expected and what "good performance" looks like. Information sessions, transparent performance evaluation criteria, and regular feedback enable employees to calibrate their effort effectively.

**Improving Instrumentality:**

- **Variable pay programmes:** Rather than relying solely on a high fixed salary, Octopus should implement variable pay linked to performance outcomes — for example: Grade A performance = 10% salary increase; Grade B = 5%; Grade C = 0%. This creates a visible, credible connection between performance and reward, directly strengthening instrumentality. Around 85% of companies globally now offer some form of variable pay (Robbins & Judge, 2024).
- **Transparent communication of performance–reward links:** Management should clearly communicate how individual performance evaluations translate into rewards. When the process is opaque or inconsistent, employees lose faith in the performance–reward relationship and instrumentality falls.

**Improving Valence:**

- **Flexible benefits:** Employees differ in what they value most — some prioritise financial rewards, others prefer career development opportunities, additional holiday, or flexible working arrangements. Offering a flexible benefits package (e.g., a "cafeteria-style" system) allows employees to select rewards that are most personally meaningful to them, maximising valence across a diverse workforce.

**Additional Complementary Practices:**

Beyond the three components of Expectancy Theory, organisations can enhance motivation through:

- **Goal-setting:** Setting specific, difficult but achievable goals — rather than vague encouragements such as "do your best" — has been shown to substantially improve task performance (Locke & Latham, 2002). Management by Objectives (MBO) extends this by having employees and managers jointly set measurable goals, increasing both commitment to those goals and clarity about what performance is required.
- **Employee involvement programmes:** Participative management — giving employees meaningful input into decisions that affect their work — increases autonomy, trust, and commitment. Employees who have a voice in how they work are more likely to feel responsible for outcomes and more motivated to achieve them.
- **Job design (Job Characteristics Model):** Jobs high in skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, and feedback generate higher levels of intrinsic motivation. In particular, autonomy gives employees a sense of responsibility for outcomes, and feedback helps them understand how effectively they are performing — both of which reinforce expectancy and instrumentality perceptions.

### Conclusion

Expectancy Theory makes clear that high salaries, while potentially beneficial to motivation through increased valence, are far from sufficient on their own. Motivation is a joint product of expectancy, instrumentality, and valence: all three must be simultaneously high for employees to be motivated to exert discretionary effort. Octopus should therefore complement its high-salary strategy with training to build employee capabilities, transparent performance management to create clear effort–performance links, and differentiated rewards tied to performance to establish credible performance–reward links. Only then will Octopus realise the full motivational potential of its compensation investment.

## Condensed Bullet Summary

**Expectancy Theory (Vroom):**
- Motivation = f(Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence) — all three must be high simultaneously
- **Expectancy:** effort → performance (can I do it?)
- **Instrumentality:** performance → reward (will I be rewarded for it?)
- **Valence:** reward → personal goals (do I care about this reward?)
- Multiplicative: if any one component is 0, motivation = 0

**Why High Salary Alone Is Insufficient:**
- High salary improves **Valence only**
- If employees doubt they can perform well → low **Expectancy**
- If performance isn't clearly linked to pay differentials → low **Instrumentality**
- High valence alone ≠ high motivation

**Additional Practices:**

*Improving Expectancy:*
- Training & development (builds self-efficacy and skills)
- Clear performance standards and feedback (effort–performance clarity)

*Improving Instrumentality:*
- Variable pay programmes (e.g., performance grades tied to % pay rise)
- Transparent communication of performance–reward links

*Improving Valence:*
- Flexible/cafeteria-style benefits (personalised rewards)

*Additional:*
- Goal-setting / MBO: specific, difficult goals; cascading objectives
- Employee involvement (participative management → autonomy, commitment)
- Job design / JCM: skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, feedback → intrinsic motivation
