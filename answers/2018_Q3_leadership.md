## Full Essay Answer

### (a) What is leadership? What are the differences between leaders and managers? [20%]

Leadership can be defined as the ability to influence a group toward the achievement of a vision or set of goals (Robbins & Judge, 2024). It has been defined in several ways and involves traits, behaviours, and other factors, but at its core, leadership is about influencing others toward a common objective. Not all leaders are managers, nor are all managers leaders — nonsanctioned leadership, the ability to influence that arises outside the formal structure of the organisation, is sometimes more important than formal influence.

Organisations need both strong leadership and strong management for optimal effectiveness. The key differences can be summarised as follows:

**Leaders** tend to:
- Create vision, originate, and develop new ideas
- Inspire trust and build commitment
- Think long-term and watch the horizon
- Ask "what" and "why"
- Challenge the status quo
- "Do the right things"

**Managers** tend to:
- Implement, copy, and maintain existing systems
- Control and administer
- Think short-term and watch the bottom line
- Ask "how" and "when"
- Accept the status quo
- "Do things right"

The distinction is important because organisations need leaders to challenge the status quo, create visions of the future, and inspire organisational members to achieve those visions. Equally, they need managers to formulate detailed plans, create efficient organisational structures, and oversee day-to-day operations. The most effective individuals in senior roles combine elements of both.

### (b) Select and describe one leadership theory in detail: Transformational Leadership [40%]

Transformational leadership is one of the most researched and influential contemporary leadership theories. Transformational leaders inspire followers to transcend their own self-interests for the good of the organisation, and they can have an extraordinary effect on their followers (Burns, 1978; Bass, 1985).

**The Full Range Leadership Model**

Transformational leadership is best understood within the **full range leadership model** (Bass & Avolio, 1994), which proposes that leadership styles vary on a continuum from passive and ineffective to active and effective. The model encompasses three broad approaches:

1. **Laissez-faire leadership** (most passive/ineffective): Literally "let it be" — involves passive abdication and avoidance of leadership responsibilities. Only 17% of employees report that their supervisors are actively engaged in developing them. Laissez-faire leadership results in destructive outcomes including social loafing, harmed interpersonal relationships, and low-quality LMX.

2. **Transactional leadership** (moderately active): Guides followers toward established goals by clarifying role and task requirements, allocating rewards and punishment where needed (contingent rewards), and intervening when standards are not met (management by exception). Contingent reward can be effective but will not get employees to go above and beyond the call of duty.

3. **Transformational leadership** (most active/effective): Goes beyond transactional leadership to inspire followers through four key components, known as the **"Four I's"**:

   - **Idealized Influence:** The leader provides vision and a sense of mission, instils pride, and gains respect and trust. They serve as role models whom followers want to emulate.
   - **Inspirational Motivation:** The leader communicates high expectations, uses symbols to focus efforts, and expresses important purposes in simple ways. They articulate an appealing vision that energises followers.
   - **Intellectual Stimulation:** The leader promotes intelligence, rationality, and careful problem solving. They encourage followers to question assumptions, think creatively, and approach old problems in new ways.
   - **Individualized Consideration:** The leader gives personal attention, treats each employee individually, and coaches and advises. They recognise that each follower has unique needs and development requirements.

**Why Transformational Leadership Is Effective**

Research has identified five mechanisms through which transformational leadership achieves its effects:

1. **Affective/attitudinal mechanism:** Promotes positive employee moods, emotions, job satisfaction, organisational commitment, and feelings of well-being.
2. **Motivational mechanism:** Employees become more confident, engaged, and willing to put in time and effort.
3. **Identification mechanism:** Employees personally identify with the leader and the leader's values and identity, as well as with the team or organisation.
4. **Social exchange mechanism:** Improves the quality of exchange and relationship between leaders and followers (i.e., LMX). Research suggests this is the primary reason leadership behaviours and styles influence outcomes.
5. **Justice enhancement mechanism:** Improves employee fairness perceptions, motivating followers to contribute more and to trust the leader, team, and organisation.

**Transformational vs. Transactional Leadership**

Crucially, transformational and transactional leadership complement each other — they are not opposing approaches. Transformational leadership *builds on* transactional leadership and produces levels of follower effort and performance beyond what transactional leadership alone can achieve. The best leaders are both transactional and transformational. Research suggests that transformational leadership is more important for group performance, OCBs, and satisfaction with the leader, whereas transactional leadership (primarily contingent reward) is more important for leader effectiveness, follower performance, and follower job satisfaction.

**Evidence and Outcomes**

The four I's collectively result in extra effort from workers, higher productivity, higher creativity, higher well-being, higher morale and satisfaction, higher organisational effectiveness, higher psychological safety, improved psychological and physical health, lower turnover, lower absenteeism, and greater organisational adaptability. A review of studies in 18 different countries found that transformational leadership may be used more often in health and security industries and in cultures that value assertiveness, egalitarianism, and lower power distance.

**Criticisms and Limitations**

Despite its strong empirical support, transformational leadership is not without criticism. Some argue that transformational leadership behaviours can be construed as manipulative and unduly grant power to inspirational people. The theory can take an emotional toll on leaders, causing exhaustion and departure from leadership positions. There are also measurement concerns — it is debated whether surveys measure actual leader behaviours or merely followers' perceptions of those behaviours. Finally, it remains unclear exactly how the four dimensions combine and which dimensions are most important for different outcomes.

### (c) Critically discuss leadership as a determinant of organisational success [40%]

Leadership is undoubtedly important for organisational success, but a critical examination reveals that its impact is often overstated, and that organisational outcomes are determined by a complex interplay of factors over which leaders have limited control.

**The Case for Leadership as Important**

Leadership matters for organisations for several fundamental reasons. First, leaders provide **direction and vision** — they articulate where the organisation should go and inspire members to work toward that vision. Transformational and charismatic leaders have been shown to enhance follower task performance, OCB, creativity, and reduce turnover. Second, leaders are essential for **motivation and coordination** — through mechanisms such as LMX, contingent rewards, and individualised consideration, leaders can motivate employees to exert discretionary effort. Third, leaders shape **organisational culture** — through ethical leadership, they establish clear values and principles that guide employee behaviour. Fourth, leaders play a critical role during **times of crisis**, providing direction, stability, and meaning when organisations face uncertainty.

**The Case Against Overemphasising Leadership**

However, there are strong reasons to temper our expectations of what leadership can achieve:

1. **The Romance of Leadership (Meindl, Ehrlich, & Dukerich, 1985):** One of the most significant challenges to the primacy of leadership comes from Meindl et al.'s work on the "romance of leadership." Their research demonstrated that people have a tendency to "romanticise" leadership — we over-attribute organisational outcomes (both successes and failures) to leaders when they may be due to factors entirely outside leaders' control. We tend to see leaders as responsible for both extremely positive and extremely negative organisational performance, regardless of the actual causal mechanisms. A worldwide study found that employees who tended to romanticise leadership were more likely to believe their own leaders were transformational, regardless of whether they actually were.

2. **Leadership as Attribution:** Attribution theory suggests that leadership may be more about perception than reality. We attribute qualities such as intelligence, outgoing personality, strong verbal skills, and aggressiveness to leaders. The attribution theory of leadership states that leadership is merely an attribution people make about other individuals. What matters may be projecting the *appearance* of being a leader rather than focusing on actual accomplishments.

3. **Substitutes for and Neutralisers of Leadership:** Organisational characteristics can sometimes make leaders' actions irrelevant. **Neutralisers** — such as employee indifference to rewards — make it impossible for leader behaviour to make any difference to follower outcomes. **Substitutes** — such as employee experience, training, professional orientation, explicit formalised goals, rigid rules and procedures, and cohesive work groups — can replace the need for a leader's support or ability to create structure. Companies like Valve Corporation and W. L. Gore have experimented with eliminating leaders entirely, achieving governance through accountability to coworkers.

4. **External Factors:** Organisational success depends on many factors beyond leadership, including market conditions, technological change, competitor actions, regulatory environments, and macroeconomic trends. A CEO's influence may be far less significant than these external forces. The JCPenney example illustrates this — despite hiring multiple "well-qualified" CEOs, the company ultimately went bankrupt, suggesting that structural and market factors may override leadership effects.

5. **Follower and Team Effects:** Modern followership theory and shared leadership theory remind us that success depends heavily on the quality and makeup of followers. Engaged followers can lead to engaged leaders, and leadership roles can be distributed across team members. The followers themselves serve as perhaps the most critical contingency in determining leadership effectiveness.

**Conclusion**

Leadership is clearly an important factor in organisational success — effective leaders can motivate, coordinate, set direction, and build trust. However, a critical perspective reveals that we often overestimate the role of leaders due to romanticisation and attribution biases. Organisational success is the product of multiple variables including the leader, the followers, the situation, the organisational structure, market conditions, and often a good measure of luck. As Robbins and Judge (2024) note, much of an organisation's success or failure is due to factors outside the influence of leadership, and glorifying leadership while not recognising that it is "incredibly messy" may be an inaccurate representation of organisational reality. The most balanced view acknowledges leadership as one important variable among many, rather than the sole or primary determinant of organisational outcomes.

## Condensed Bullet Summary

### (a) Definition & Leaders vs Managers [20%]
- **Definition:** ability to influence a group toward the achievement of a vision or set of goals
- Not all leaders are managers; not all managers are leaders; nonsanctioned leadership exists
- **Leaders:** create, originate, develop, inspire trust, think long-term, ask what/why, challenge status quo, "do the right things"
- **Managers:** implement, copy, maintain, control, think short-term, ask how/when, accept status quo, "do things right"

### (b) Transformational Leadership Theory [40%]
- Part of the **full range leadership model** (Bass & Avolio, 1994): laissez-faire → transactional → transformational
- **Laissez-faire:** passive abdication; most ineffective
- **Transactional:** contingent rewards + management by exception; effective but limited
- **Transformational:** inspires followers to transcend self-interest; the **Four I's:**
  - Idealized Influence (role model, vision, trust)
  - Inspirational Motivation (high expectations, symbols, vision)
  - Intellectual Stimulation (creativity, problem-solving)
  - Individualized Consideration (personal attention, coaching)
- **Five mechanisms:** affective, motivational, identification, social exchange (primary), justice enhancement
- Builds *on top of* transactional leadership, not opposed to it
- **Outcomes:** higher performance, creativity, satisfaction, OCBs, well-being; lower turnover, absenteeism
- **Criticisms:** can be manipulative; emotionally exhausting for leaders; measurement issues (behaviours vs perceptions); unclear how dimensions combine

### (c) Leadership & Organisational Success [40%]

**For leadership mattering:**
- Vision and direction
- Motivation and coordination (LMX, rewards)
- Shapes organisational culture
- Critical in times of crisis

**Against overemphasising leadership:**
- **Romance of Leadership** (Meindl, Ehrlich, & Dukerich, 1985): we over-attribute outcomes to leaders
- **Attribution theory:** leadership may be more perception than reality
- **Substitutes/neutralisers:** experience, training, formalised goals, cohesive groups can replace leader effects
- **External factors:** market conditions, technology, competition, regulation
- **Followership:** success depends on follower quality; shared leadership possible

**Balanced conclusion:** Leadership is one important variable among many, not the sole determinant; we must guard against romanticisation
