## Full Essay Answer

### (a) What is personality? What are the factors that determine an individual's personality? [20%]

Personality can be defined as the sum total of ways in which an individual reacts to and interacts with the world around them (Robbins & Judge, 2024). It is most often described in terms of measurable personality traits — enduring characteristics that an individual frequently exhibits across many situations and that remain relatively stable over time. The more consistent a characteristic is over time and across situations, the more important that trait is in describing the individual.

The factors determining an individual's personality are primarily **hereditary** (genetic), with a secondary role played by **environmental** factors. Research on twins raised apart has consistently demonstrated that genetics account for approximately 50% of personality variation, and that twins raised in different households display remarkably similar personality profiles. This suggests that a significant portion of personality is inherited.

Environmental factors — including the culture one grows up in, early conditioning, family norms, friends, and social groups — also shape personality, but they tend to exert their influence primarily during formative years. Notably, research suggests that ageing has limited effects on personality; the basic personality structure tends to stabilise by early adulthood. Additionally, our culture influences the way we describe ourselves and others — although many personality traits do tend to emerge across cultures, certain unique traits may appear in specific cultural contexts. For instance, research in Chinese contexts has uncovered unique traits focused on interpersonal relatedness.

### (b) Compare and contrast the MBTI and the Big Five Personality Model [40%]

The two most widely used and best-known personality frameworks are the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) and the Big Five Model. Both attempt to describe a person's total personality through exploration of its facets, but they differ fundamentally in their scientific rigour, approach to measurement, and practical applications.

**The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI)** is the most widely used personality assessment instrument in the world. It is a 100-question personality test that classifies respondents along four dichotomous dimensions:

- **Extroverted (E) vs. Introverted (I):** Extroverts are outgoing, sociable, and assertive; introverts are quiet and shy.
- **Sensing (S) vs. Intuitive (N):** Sensing types are practical and prefer routine and order, focusing on details; intuitive types rely on unconscious processes and look at the big picture.
- **Thinking (T) vs. Feeling (F):** Thinking types use reason and logic; feeling types rely on personal values and emotions.
- **Judging (J) vs. Perceiving (P):** Judging types want control and structure; perceiving types are flexible and spontaneous.

These four dimensions combine to produce 16 personality types (e.g., INTJ, ESTJ). The MBTI is valued for increasing self-awareness and is extensively used in corporate training and team-building.

**The Big Five Personality Model** proposes five basic dimensions that encompass most of the differences in human personality:

- **Conscientiousness:** Personal consistency and reliability; responsible, organised, dependable, and persistent.
- **Emotional Stability:** The ability to withstand stress; calm, self-confident, and secure (versus nervous, anxious, and insecure).
- **Extraversion:** Relational approach toward the social world; gregarious, assertive, and sociable.
- **Openness to Experience:** Range of interests and fascination with novelty; creative, curious, and artistically sensitive.
- **Agreeableness:** Propensity to defer to others; cooperative, warm, and trusting.

**Key Similarities:**

Both frameworks attempt to provide a comprehensive description of personality. There is dimensional overlap — for example, both include an extraversion-introversion dimension. Both are used in organisational settings to understand individual differences.

**Key Differences:**

1. **Scientific validity:** The MBTI was developed based on Carl Jung's neo-Freudian theories in a rather unscientific, subjective manner. Very little empirical evidence supports the theory it is based on. As one academic noted, "Jung's theories are not considered to be solid. He wasn't an empiricist. He didn't collect data." Evidence does not support the validity of the MBTI as a personality measure — it has been compared to horoscopes in terms of scientific validity. The Big Five, by contrast, is built on a solid foundation of decades of empirical research, with test scores doing a strong job of predicting how people behave in a variety of real-life situations.

2. **Measurement approach:** The MBTI forces people into one type or another on each dimension — you are either extroverted or introverted, with no middle ground. This is problematic because personality traits exist on a continuum. The Big Five measures traits on a spectrum, acknowledging that most people fall somewhere between the extremes.

3. **Test-retest reliability:** When people retake the MBTI, they often receive different results, undermining its consistency. The Big Five traits remain relatively stable for an individual over time, with only some daily variations.

4. **Predictive power:** The MBTI is unrelated to job performance. The Big Five, on the other hand, has demonstrable links to important organisational outcomes — conscientiousness is the strongest predictor of job performance, extraversion predicts leadership emergence, and emotional stability relates to job and life satisfaction.

5. **Primary application:** The MBTI is used mostly in practice (corporate training, team-building, self-awareness workshops), while the Big Five is the predominant framework for empirical research in organisational behaviour and psychology.

In summary, while the MBTI remains popular due to its simplicity and non-judgmental framing, evidence-based practitioners and managers would be well advised to consider the Big Five over the MBTI for any decisions relating to selection, development, or predicting organisational outcomes.

### (c) Why does personality matter in organisations? [40%]

Personality matters in organisations because individual traits have demonstrable links to key organisational outcomes
including job performance, leadership, satisfaction, and teamwork.                                                  
                                                                                                           
The Big Five traits each predict specific outcomes. Conscientiousness is the strongest overall predictor of job      
performance — highly conscientious employees develop greater job knowledge, exhibit more organisational citizenship
behaviours (OCBs), and engage in fewer counterproductive work behaviours (CWBs). Extraversion predicts leadership    
emergence and career success, primarily through the positive emotions extroverts experience and their social
adeptness. Emotional stability is most strongly linked to job and life satisfaction and reduced burnout. Openness
predicts creativity, innovation, and adaptability to organisational change. Agreeableness predicts performance in
interpersonal roles such as customer service, though at the cost of lower career earnings due to reduced
assertiveness.

Beyond individual performance, personality shapes person-organisation fit (Schneider, 1987). People are attracted to,
selected by, and retained by organisations that match their values — and leave those that do not. Over time, this
attraction-selection-attrition cycle produces relative personality homogeneity within organisations, directly shaping
organisational culture. Holland's personality-job fit theory further demonstrates that congruence between
personality type and occupational environment predicts job satisfaction and reduces turnover.

In conclusion, personality is not merely an academic concept — it influences individual performance, team dynamics,  
leadership, and organisational culture, making it one of the most practically significant variables in organisational
behaviour.

## Condensed Bullet Summary

### (a) Definition & Determinants [20%]
- **Definition:** sum total of ways an individual reacts to and interacts with the world (Robbins & Judge)
- **Personality traits:** enduring characteristics exhibited consistently across situations
- **Determinants:** primarily heredity/genetics (~50%); also environment (culture, upbringing, social groups)
- Ageing has limited effects; personality stabilises in early adulthood
- Cultural differences exist in which traits emerge

### (b) MBTI vs Big Five [40%]
**MBTI:**
- 4 dimensions: E/I, S/N, T/F, J/P → 16 types
- Most widely used personality assessment globally
- Based on Jung's unscientific theories; little empirical support
- Forces binary classification (no middle ground)
- Poor test-retest reliability
- Unrelated to job performance
- Used mainly for self-awareness, corporate training

**Big Five:**
- 5 dimensions: Conscientiousness, Emotional Stability, Extraversion, Openness, Agreeableness
- Strong empirical foundation across decades of research
- Measures traits on a continuum
- Stable over time
- Predicts job performance, leadership, satisfaction
- Predominant framework in academic research

**Overlap:** Both include extraversion dimension; both describe total personality
**Key difference:** MBTI = popular but unscientific; Big Five = empirically validated and predictive

### (c) Why Personality Matters [40%]
- **Conscientiousness** → strongest predictor of job performance; higher OCBs, lower CWBs; but extremes can reduce creativity
- **Extraversion** → predicts leadership emergence; career advantage via social skills and positive emotions
- **Emotional Stability** → strongest link to life/job satisfaction; reduced burnout
- **Openness** → creativity, innovation, leadership effectiveness, adaptability
- **Agreeableness** → better in interpersonal roles, more OCBs; but lower career earnings
- **Person-organisation fit** → people attracted to, selected by, and retained by organisations matching their values
- **ASA cycle** (Schneider, 1987) → leads to personality homogeneity within organisations
- **Holland's personality-job fit** → congruence between personality type and occupation predicts satisfaction
- **Other attributes:** CSEs (ambitious goals, better performance), self-monitoring (leadership emergence), proactive personality (initiative, career success)
- **Barrick & Mount (1991)** meta-analysis: validated Big Five-performance links across occupations
